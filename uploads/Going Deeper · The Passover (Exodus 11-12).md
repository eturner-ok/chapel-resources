# Going Deeper · The Passover (Exodus 11–12)

Nov 6, 2026 · @Eric Turner

On one spring night in Egypt, families ate a hurried dinner with their sandals on and their staffs in hand. Outside, blood was drying on their doorframes, and they were waiting for God to do what he had said. That night raises some of the hardest questions students ask about the Bible, and it points straight to the cross.&#32;

## The Night

### 1. Why Did God Strike Egypt’s Firstborn?

The short answer: the tenth plague is a showdown over whose sons matter, and God takes the side of the families Pharaoh treated as disposable.&#32;&#32;

It was Pharaoh who first ordered the Hebrew midwives to kill newborn boys (1:16), and when that failed, he commanded that every Hebrew son be thrown into the Nile (1:22). Before Moses ever went back to Egypt, God told him how this would end: “Israel is my firstborn son… If you refuse to let him go, behold, I will kill your firstborn son” (4:22–23). The text first records that warning being delivered to Pharaoh in 11:4–8, after nine plagues that grew more severe each time.

The Hebrew word for Egypt’s “great cry” (11:6; 12:30) is the same word used for Israel’s cry under slavery (3:7, 9). The cry has reversed. In this plague, God also says he will go out himself, “about midnight I will go out in the midst of Egypt” (11:4; compare 12:12). The earlier plagues came through Moses’ or Aaron’s staff and hand, and this one has no staff in it at all. “Firstborn” meant the oldest son at any age, “from the firstborn of Pharaoh who sits on his throne” to the son of a servant at the handmill (11:5). Pharaoh’s heir was seen in Egypt as a god in waiting, which is part of why God calls this night judgment “on all the gods of Egypt” (12:12).

The door also stayed open. Some of Pharaoh’s own officials took God’s warnings seriously (9:20), and “a mixed multitude” of non-Israelites left Egypt with Israel (12:38).

What happens next shows what the night was about. Right after the exodus, God claims every firstborn in Israel as his own (13:2). Each firstborn son had to be “redeemed,” bought back with a price. Exodus ties that law directly to this night: the LORD killed Egypt’s firstborn, “but all the firstborn of my sons I redeem” (13:15). Later the Levites stood in for the firstborn (Num 3:12–13), and Mary and Joseph kept this same law for Jesus (Luke 2:22–23).

*It’s ok to say:* “This is one of the hardest parts of the Bible. God was standing up for families Pharaoh hurt, and he warned Pharaoh again and again. It’s still heavy, and the Bible lets it be heavy.”

**Connecting the Dots:** Students notice fast when adults treat some people as if they don’t matter. Exodus answers that God notices too. The families Pharaoh tried to erase, God calls “my firstborn son.”

### 2. How Could God Harden Pharaoh’s Heart and Still Hold Him Responsible?

Exodus describes the hardening in more than one way, and that’s the key to this question. Sometimes Pharaoh hardens his own heart (8:15, 32). Sometimes the text simply says his heart was hardened (7:13). And sometimes God hardens it (9:12; 10:1, 20, 27; 11:10), just as he told Moses he would (4:21; 7:3). The theme shows up about twenty times across the story.

Read together, those descriptions hold two things at once: Pharaoh is guilty, and God is in control. Pharaoh chose “no” first and kept choosing it, even after relief came (8:15). Over time, God hardened a heart that was already hard. In Exodus, a hard heart means arrogance that stops listening to reason and stops feeling compassion.

One of the Hebrew words for hardening literally means “heavy,” and Egyptian readers may have caught why that matters. In the Egyptian Book of the Dead, a person’s heart is weighed on a scale against a feather representing Maat, the Egyptian idea of truth and justice. A heavy heart meant doom. Exodus tells us Pharaoh’s heart was heavy.

Paul picks up this story in Romans 9:17–18, and Christians have debated how God’s sovereignty and Pharaoh’s choices fit together ever since. It’s fine to leave some of that unresolved.

*It’s ok to say:* “We don’t understand everything about how this works. Pharaoh kept saying no to God, and after a while God let that no become who he was.”

**Connecting the Dots:** Habits of the heart form early. A student who keeps brushing past a conscience is practicing Pharaoh’s pattern, one small “no” at a time. The same pattern runs the other way, too: every “yes” to God makes the next one easier.

### 3. What Did the Blood Actually Do?

Everyone agrees on this much: the blood was a sign of trust, and God honored it. “The blood shall be a sign for you… and when I see the blood, I will pass over you” (12:13). Beyond that, Christians read it in two main ways, and most evangelical teaching lands on the first.

God didn’t need the blood to find Israel. In earlier plagues he had already kept them safe with no marker at all, sparing their land and their livestock (8:22–23; 9:4, 26; 10:23). This was the first plague where Israel had to do something to be spared, and what they did was obey a word they couldn’t yet see come true. Hebrews puts it simply: “By faith he kept the Passover and sprinkled the blood” (Heb 11:28).

**The first reading: a substitute.** Exodus 12:30 says there was not a house in Egypt “where someone was not dead.” One evangelical commentator points out that this was true of Israel’s houses too, because the lamb had died there. The lamb was even sized to the household (12:4), an exact match for the people it covered. God had called Israel his firstborn (4:22), and on this reading, God’s firstborn lived because the lamb died in their place. The New Testament reads Jesus’ death through this lens: “Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed” (1 Cor 5:7).

**The second reading: protection, not a sin offering.** Other scholars point out that chapter 12 never mentions sin, and the lamb is eaten as a family meal. Another evangelical commentator writes that Passover was “scarcely a sacrifice in the later sense,” though it still represents a life laid down. Some read the blood as cleansing the household and marking it as belonging to God. BibleProject’s short video “What Is Passover?” takes that approach.

Both readings see a life laid down and God protecting everyone under the blood. The text itself calls it “the sacrifice of the LORD’s Passover” (12:27), and the New Testament leans hard on the first reading, which is why most evangelical teaching treats the Passover lamb as a picture of substitution.

This was never magic. Other ancient peoples painted doorways to scare off evil spirits, but in Exodus the blood had no power of its own. It was a sign of a family trusting God.

*It’s ok to say:* “God saved them. The blood was how they showed they trusted him to.”

**Connecting the Dots:** Israel’s faith that night looked like a bundle of hyssop and a bowl. For most students, faith will look just as small and physical: one right choice, made because God said so, before they can see how it turns out.

## The Words and the Meal

### 4. What Does “Passover” Mean?

Probably “protect.” The traditional translation, “pass over,” isn’t wrong, but it misses something.

The Hebrew word is *pesach*, and scholars have suggested a few meanings for it. The traditional one, behind the English word “Passover,” is to skip or pass by. A second meaning comes from Isaiah, who uses the same verb for God defending Jerusalem: “Like birds hovering, so the LORD of hosts will protect Jerusalem; he will protect and deliver it; he will spare and rescue it” (Isa 31:5). Some of the oldest Jewish translations went a third direction and rendered it “have compassion.”

Exodus 12:23 fits the “protect” reading well. The LORD “will pass over the door and will not allow the destroyer to enter your houses.” The picture there is God standing guard at the door rather than simply skipping a house. Who “the destroyer” is stays debated. Some see an angel (compare 2 Sam 24:16), some see the plague itself, and 12:12 makes clear that God is the one acting.

The Greek form of the word, *pascha*, gives us the English word “paschal,” and in many languages the word for Easter comes from it, like *Pascua* in Spanish and *Pâques* in French.

**A new calendar.** The same chapter resets Israel’s year: “This month shall be for you the beginning of months” (12:2). The month was called Abib, meaning “ripe barley,” and was later known as Nisan. Because months followed the moon, the 14th fell at the full moon, so the night they left was bright enough to travel by. Israel’s year now began with the night God rescued them.

**Connecting the Dots:** Ask students what “Passover” means and most will say “God skipped their houses.” The “protect” reading gives them something better: a God who stays at the door all night.

### 5. What Was on the Table?

A roasted lamb or young goat, flat bread, and bitter herbs, eaten in a hurry by a family dressed to leave.

**The lamb.** The Hebrew word *seh* can mean a lamb or a young goat. It had to be a year-old male with no defect, chosen on the 10th and kept until the 14th (12:3–6). Small households shared one with their neighbors (12:4). It was roasted whole over the fire, “its head with its legs and its inner parts,” not eaten raw or boiled (12:9). Anything left by morning was burned (12:10), and none of its bones could be broken (12:46).

**The bread.** Matzah is bread made without yeast. When Pharaoh finally said go, Israel left before the dough could rise and carried it in kneading bowls wrapped in their cloaks (12:34, 39). Ancient leaven was a lump of old fermented dough saved from the last batch, a lot like sourdough starter. Clearing all of it out for seven days (12:15) meant a clean break and a fresh start. Many Christians read leaven as a picture of sin, but that comes from Paul, not Exodus. In 1 Corinthians 5:6–8, Paul uses it for a specific case of sexual immorality the church in Corinth was tolerating.

**The bitter herbs.** Exodus 1:14 says the Egyptians “made their lives bitter with hard service.” The word for “bitter herbs” in 12:8 comes from the same Hebrew root. Every bite tasted like the slavery they were about to leave.

**The posture.** They ate “with your belt fastened, your sandals on your feet, and your staff in your hand” (12:11). The text doesn’t say they stood, but a staff in hand points that way. By Jesus’ day the posture had flipped. He and his disciples “reclined at table” (Matt 26:20), the way free people ate. Later Jewish tradition required even the poorest person to recline at Passover, and it treated the haste of that first night as belonging only to the Passover in Egypt.

## The Memory

### 6. Israel Kept Forgetting

For a festival God commanded “as a statute forever” (12:14), Israel kept Passover surprisingly rarely, and the Bible is honest about it.

Outside Exodus, the Old Testament records only a handful of Passovers. Israel kept one in the wilderness (Num 9:1–5) and one when they entered the land under Joshua (Josh 5:10). After that, the record goes quiet for centuries, until the reforms of King Hezekiah (2 Chr 30) and King Josiah (2 Kgs 23:21–23; 2 Chr 35), with one more after the return from exile (Ezra 6:19–22). When Josiah held his Passover, the writer adds that nothing like it had been kept “since the days of the judges” (2 Kgs 23:22). Hezekiah admits the people “had not kept it as often as prescribed” (2 Chr 30:5). Josiah’s Passover even came after his workers found a forgotten scroll of God’s law in the Temple (2 Kgs 22:8). Israel had lost track of the very instructions that told them to remember.

God saw that coming and built grace into the rules. Numbers 9:6–12 allowed a make-up Passover a month later for anyone who was ceremonially unclean or traveling, and Hezekiah used that exact provision (2 Chr 30:2). Even then, many people ate without being properly purified. Hezekiah prayed, “May the good LORD pardon everyone who sets his heart to seek God… even though not according to the sanctuary’s rules of cleanness.” The next line says, “And the LORD heard Hezekiah and healed the people” (2 Chr 30:18–20).

**Connecting the Dots:** Forgetting God is an old problem, and remembering takes effort. God expected his people to drift and built second chances into the very festival meant to keep them anchored. Students who feel like they’ve drifted need to hear that the door back is already open.

## The Lamb

### 7. Did Jesus Eat the Passover or Become It?

The Gospels show both. Matthew, Mark, and Luke present the Last Supper as a Passover meal. John places Jesus’ death on the day the Passover lambs were being slaughtered. Christians explain the difference in more than one way, and every explanation lands in the same place.

In Matthew, Mark, and Luke, the disciples prepare “to eat the Passover” (Mark 14:12), and Jesus says, “I have earnestly desired to eat this Passover with you before I suffer” (Luke 22:15). In John, the religious leaders won’t enter Pilate’s headquarters “so that they would not be defiled, but could eat the Passover” (John 18:28), and the crucifixion happens on “the day of Preparation of the Passover” (19:14). John then points out that Jesus’ bones weren’t broken, fulfilling Exodus 12:46 (John 19:36).

There are three common ways to handle this. Some scholars think John arranged his timeline to make a theological point, showing Jesus as the true Passover lamb. Others say historians simply can’t settle which timeline is exact from the evidence we have. Many evangelical scholars argue the accounts fit together, since “Passover” could name the whole week-long festival and “Preparation” was the usual word for Friday. Whichever view a teacher holds, scholars broadly agree that Passover is the right backdrop for understanding the Last Supper.

*It’s ok to say:* “The Gospels tell it from different angles, and Christians have explained the timing a few ways. All four agree on the big thing: Jesus died at Passover, and the New Testament calls him our Passover lamb.”

**What Jesus said at the table.** Jesus took Passover bread and said, “This is my body” (Luke 22:19). Over the cup he said, “this is my blood of the covenant” (Mark 14:24), echoing the words Moses spoke when God made his covenant with Israel at Sinai (Exod 24:8). In Luke, he calls it “the new covenant in my blood” (Luke 22:20), echoing God’s promise of a new covenant (Jer 31:31). The Supper ties Passover and Sinai to that promise in one meal. Afterward, they sang a hymn and went out to the Mount of Olives (Mark 14:26). Jewish tradition sang Psalms 113–118 at Passover, so that’s very likely what Jesus sang the night he was arrested, including “The stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone” (Ps 118:22).

**The lamb through Scripture.** Isaac asks, “Where is the lamb?” and Abraham answers, “God will provide for himself the lamb” (Gen 22:7–8). In Egypt, every household has one. Isaiah describes God’s servant as “a lamb that is led to the slaughter” (Isa 53:7), and John the Baptist points to Jesus as “the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world” (John 1:29). Paul writes almost in passing, “Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed” (1 Cor 5:7), which suggests the idea was already familiar to the earliest Christians.

**A test for connections like these.** The strongest connections between the Old Testament and Jesus are the ones the New Testament itself makes, like the unblemished lamb, the unbroken bones, and “Christ, our Passover lamb.” Popular material sometimes goes further, claiming the blood on the doorframe formed a cross or that later Seder customs secretly picture Jesus. Those are devotional readings added much later, not part of the original story.

**Connecting the Dots:** When Jesus wanted his followers to understand the cross, he handed them bread and a cup at a Passover table. He pointed them back to the night a lamb died so God’s people could go free.

### 8. From the Passover Table to the Lord’s Table

Jesus started the Lord’s Supper at a Passover meal, and Communion carries some of Passover’s shape without repeating it.

**What carries over.** Both meals are about remembering. God called Passover “a memorial day” (Exod 12:14), and Jesus said, “Do this in remembrance of me” (Luke 22:19). In both, God’s people eat together as the people he rescued, and in both, the meal points forward. Jesus told his disciples he wouldn’t drink the cup again “until that day when I drink it new with you in my Father’s kingdom” (Matt 26:29), and later Jewish tradition looked for God’s final rescue to come on Passover night. Paul says that every time Christians take the Supper, “you proclaim the Lord’s death until he comes” (1 Cor 11:26).

**What’s different.** There’s no lamb at the Lord’s Table, because Jesus is the lamb. Churches take Communion many times a year, while Passover came once. And when Paul gives instructions for the Supper in 1 Corinthians 11, he doesn’t tie it to Passover. He uses Passover imagery separately, back in chapter 5.

**Where Christians differ.** Christian traditions understand what happens at the Table in different ways. Many Baptist and nondenominational churches see it mainly as a remembrance. Reformed churches teach that Christ is spiritually present as believers receive it. Lutheran, Catholic, and Orthodox churches hold that Christ is truly present in the bread and wine. Families at our school come from several of these traditions, so it helps to speak about what they share: the Supper looks back to the cross and forward to Jesus’ return.

*It’s ok to say:* “Jesus started Communion at a Passover meal. Passover remembered God bringing Israel out of Egypt, and Communion remembers Jesus’ death and looks forward to his return.”

**A word on “Christian Seders.”** Some churches host Passover Seders that present the modern Seder as what Jesus did at the Last Supper. The Seder Jewish families celebrate today, with its set order and its four cups of wine, took shape after the Romans destroyed the Temple in AD 70. Much of it is recorded in the Mishnah, a collection of Jewish teaching from around AD 200. These events can be meaningful, but it’s best not to claim Jesus followed that script. Jewish neighbors still celebrate Passover today, and our language about it should be respectful.

**Connecting the Dots:** Communion is remembering together. Students who take it at their home church are doing what Israel did at Passover: telling the story of a rescue they didn’t earn, at a table God set.

## A Hard History

### 9. When a Night of Watching Became a Night of Fear

For centuries, Christians made Passover night dangerous for Jewish communities, and every Christian teacher should know it.

Exodus calls Passover “a night of watching by the LORD” (12:42), a night God guarded and Israel would keep every year after. A Jewish commentator on Exodus notes the tragic twist that followed. Across Christian Europe in the Middle Ages, and in some places into the twentieth century, Passover became a night when Jewish families kept watch against their neighbors. The reason was the “blood libel,” a lie that Jews used Christian blood in their Passover rituals. Crowds, often stirred up by clergy, attacked Jewish communities because of it.

The accusation was false from the start. The Torah forbids eating any blood at all (Lev 17:10–14), and the Passover it describes remembers God protecting the vulnerable, not harming them. Jesus himself kept Passover, and his first followers were Jewish.

Here we see why the way we talk about other people’s faith matters. When Passover comes up, speak about Jewish neighbors and their celebration with respect, and steer away from framing Jewish practice as empty or legalistic.

### Box: Did This Really Happen?

There’s no Egyptian record of the exodus, but that’s less surprising than it sounds. Egyptian inscriptions tended to celebrate victories, not record disasters. The earliest mention of Israel outside the Bible is an Egyptian victory monument from Pharaoh Merneptah in the late 1200s BC, which already names Israel as a people living in Canaan.

Two questions stay open among Bible-believing scholars. The first is the date. First Kings 6:1 points to the mid-1400s BC, while much of the outside evidence fits the 1200s, during the reign of Ramesses the Great. The second is the size. Exodus 12:37 counts about 600,000 men, which would mean two million people or more. The Hebrew word for “thousand” (*elef*) can also mean a clan or a military unit, which would make the group much smaller, and both readings have faithful defenders.

Some details point toward a real memory. A nation inventing its own origin story would rarely make its ancestors slaves. And several names in Moses’ own tribe, like Moses and Phinehas, appear to be Egyptian, which fits a people who lived in Egypt for generations.

*It’s ok to say:* “We don’t have an Egyptian record of it, but the evidence we do have fits the story, and Israel had no reason to invent being slaves.”

## The Takeaway

That night, every house in Egypt had a death in it. The only question was whether it was the lamb. God kept his promise to Abraham to the very day, and centuries later Jesus sat down at a Passover table and used its bread and cup to explain his own death.
