The Passover

Exodus 11–12
Nov 18, 2026 · Mike O'Hara
← Teacher Notes Inside the House →

On one spring night in Egypt, families ate a hurried dinner with their sandals on and their staffs in hand. Outside, blood was drying on their doorframes, and they were waiting for God to do what he had said. That night raises some of the hardest questions students ask about the Bible, and it points straight to the cross.

The Night · Question 1

Why Did God Strike Egypt’s Firstborn?

The tenth plague is a showdown over whose sons matter, and God takes the side of the families Pharaoh treated as disposable.

It was Pharaoh who first ordered the Hebrew midwives to kill newborn boys (1:16), and when that failed, he commanded that every Hebrew son be thrown into the Nile (1:22). Before Moses ever went back to Egypt, God told him how this would end: “Israel is my firstborn son… If you refuse to let him go, behold, I will kill your firstborn son” (4:22–23). The text first records that warning being delivered to Pharaoh in 11:4–8, after nine plagues that grew more severe each time.

“Israel is my firstborn son…”Exodus 4:22–23

The Hebrew word for Egypt’s “great cry” (11:6; 12:30) is the same word used for Israel’s cry under slavery (3:7, 9). The cry has reversed. In this plague, God also says he will go out himself, “about midnight I will go out in the midst of Egypt” (11:4; compare 12:12). The earlier plagues came through Moses’ or Aaron’s staff and hand, but this one has no staff in it at all. “Firstborn” meant the oldest son at any age, “from the firstborn of Pharaoh who sits on his throne” to the son of a servant at the handmill (11:5). Pharaoh’s heir was seen in Egypt as a god in waiting, which is part of why God calls this night judgment “on all the gods of Egypt” (12:12).

The door also stayed open to outsiders. Some of Pharaoh’s own officials took God’s warnings seriously and brought their servants and livestock inside before the hail (9:20). When Israel finally walked out, they didn’t walk out alone. Exodus says “a mixed multitude also went up with them” (12:38), people who weren’t Hebrews but chose to trust Israel’s God and leave with his people. Right away God made room for them. Any foreigner willing to join the community could keep the Passover and be “as a native of the land” (12:48–49). The rescue was never only for one bloodline. From the start, God was keeping his promise that through Abraham “all the families of the earth shall be blessed” (Gen 12:3).

What happens next shows what the night was about. Long before Passover, God had called Israel “my firstborn son” (4:22). In the ancient world, the firstborn son was the heir. He carried the family name and the family’s future. By calling Israel his firstborn, God was saying this nation belonged to him and he would fight for it. Pharaoh had enslaved God’s firstborn and drowned their baby boys. On Passover night, God answered by striking Egypt’s firstborn while sparing Israel’s, because a lamb died in their place.

Right after the exodus, God made sure Israel would never forget it. He told them every firstborn son now belonged to him (13:2), because he had spared those sons that night. A family couldn’t simply keep their oldest son as if nothing had happened. They had to “redeem” him, which means buy him back. Parents paid a price, later set at five shekels of silver, when the baby was a month old (Num 18:15–16). Exodus ties the law directly to this night: the LORD killed Egypt’s firstborn, “but all the firstborn of my sons I redeem” (13:15). When a child asked why the family did this, the parents were to tell the story of Passover (13:14–15). Every oldest son in Israel grew up knowing he was alive because a price had been paid for him.

That law reaches all the way to Jesus. When he was born, Mary and Joseph brought him to the Temple and presented him as a firstborn son, just as the law required (Luke 2:22–23). The baby who was bought back that day would grow up to pay the price himself. Jesus said he came “to give his life as a ransom for many” (Mark 10:45), and he died at Passover. Israel’s firstborn lived because a lamb died in their place. Jesus is that lamb, and everyone who trusts him is bought back by his life.

It’s ok to say

“This is one of the hardest parts of the Bible. God was standing up for families Pharaoh hurt, and he warned Pharaoh again and again. It’s still heavy, and the Bible lets it be heavy.”

Connecting the Dots

There is a deep sense of injustice when people treat others as if they don’t matter. Exodus shows us that God notices too. The families Pharaoh tried to erase, God calls “my firstborn son.”
The Night · Question 2

How Could God Harden Pharaoh’s Heart and Still Hold Him Responsible?

Exodus describes the hardening in more than one way, and that’s the key to this question.

Pharaoh

Sometimes Pharaoh hardens his own heart (8:15, 32).

No one named

Sometimes the text simply says his heart was hardened (7:13).

God

And sometimes God hardens it (9:12; 10:1, 20, 27; 11:10), just as he told Moses he would (4:21; 7:3).

The theme shows up about twenty times across the story.

Read together, those descriptions hold two things at once: Pharaoh is guilty, and God is in control. Pharaoh chose “no” first and kept choosing it, even after relief came (8:15). Over time, God hardened a heart that was already hard. In Exodus, a hard heart means arrogance that stops listening to reason and stops feeling compassion.

Paul picks up this in Romans 9:17–18, and Christians have debated how God’s sovereignty and Pharaoh’s choices fit together ever since. It’s fine to leave some of that unresolved.

It’s ok to say

“We don’t understand everything about how this works. What we do know is Pharaoh kept saying no to God, and after a while God let that no become who he was.”

Connecting the Dots

Habits of the heart form early. A student who keeps brushing past a conscience is practicing Pharaoh’s pattern, one small “no” at a time. The same pattern runs the other way, too: every “yes” to God makes the next one easier.
The Night · Question 3

What Did the Blood Actually Do?

There is some debate over this but the one thing they agree on is the blood was a sign of trust, and God honored it.

“The blood shall be a sign for you… and when I see the blood, I will pass over you” (12:13). Beyond that, Christians read it in two main ways, and most evangelical teaching lands on the first.

God didn’t need the blood to know which houses were theirs. In earlier plagues he had already kept them safe with no marker at all, sparing their land and their livestock (8:22–23; 9:4, 26; 10:23). This was the first plague where Israel had to do something to be spared, and what they did was obey a word they couldn’t yet see come true. Hebrews puts it simply: “By faith he kept the Passover and sprinkled the blood” (Heb 11:28).

Exodus 12:30 says there was not a house in Egypt “where someone was not dead.” One evangelical commentator points out that this was true of Israel’s houses too, because the lamb had died there. The lamb was even sized to the household (12:4), an exact match for the people it covered. God had called Israel his firstborn (4:22), and on this reading, God’s firstborn lived because the lamb died in their place. The New Testament reads Jesus’ death through this lens: “Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed” (1 Cor 5:7).

Other scholars point out that chapter 12 never mentions sin, and the lamb is eaten as a family meal. Another evangelical commentator writes that Passover was “scarcely a sacrifice in the later sense,” though it still represents a life laid down. Some read the blood as cleansing the household and marking it as belonging to God. BibleProject’s short video “What Is Passover?” takes that approach.
Common ground

Both interpretations see a life laid down and God protecting everyone under the blood. The text itself calls it “the sacrifice of the LORD’s Passover” (12:27), and the New Testament leans hard on the first reading, which is why most evangelical teaching treats the Passover lamb as a picture of substitution.

Other ancient peoples painted doorways to scare off evil spirits, believing there was magic and power in the action itself.  But in Exodus the blood had no power of its own. It was simply a sign of a family trusting God.

THE SIMPLIST WAY TO SAY IT:

“God saved them and the blood was how they showed they trusted him to.”

Connecting the Dots

Israel’s faith that night looked like a bundle of hyssop and a bowl. For most students, faith will look just as small and physical: one right choice, made because God said so, before they can see how it turns out.
The Words and the Meal · Question 4

What Does “Passover” Mean?

Probably “protect.” The traditional translation, “pass over,” isn’t wrong, but it misses something.

The Hebrew word is pesach, and scholars have suggested a few meanings for it.

pass over

The traditional one, behind the English word “Passover,” is to skip or pass by.

protectProbably

A second meaning comes from Isaiah, who uses the same verb for God defending Jerusalem: “Like birds hovering, so the LORD of hosts will protect Jerusalem; he will protect and deliver it; he will spare and rescue it” (Isa 31:5).

have compassion

Some of the oldest Jewish translations went a third direction and rendered it “have compassion.”

Exodus 12:23 fits the “protect” reading well. The LORD “will pass over the door and will not allow the destroyer to enter your houses.” The picture there is God standing guard at the door rather than simply skipping a house. Who “the destroyer” is stays debated. Some see an angel (compare 2 Sam 24:16), some see the plague itself, and 12:12 makes clear that God is the one acting.

A new calendar.

The same chapter resets Israel’s year: “This month shall be for you the beginning of months” (12:2). The month was called Abib, meaning “ripe barley,” and was later known as Nisan. Because months followed the moon, the 14th fell at the full moon, so the night they left was bright enough to travel by. Israel’s year now began with the night God rescued them.

Connecting the Dots

Ask students what “Passover” means and most will say “God skipped their houses.” The “protect” reading gives them something better: a God who stays at the door all night.
The Words and the Meal · Question 5

What Was on the Table?

A roasted lamb or young goat, flat bread, and bitter herbs, eaten in a hurry by a family dressed to leave.

Tap each part of the meal

The lamb. The Hebrew word seh can mean a lamb or a young goat. It had to be a year-old male with no defect, chosen on the 10th and kept until the 14th (12:3–6). Small households shared one with their neighbors (12:4). It was roasted whole over the fire, “its head with its legs and its inner parts,” not eaten raw or boiled (12:9). Anything left by morning was burned (12:10), and none of its bones could be broken (12:46).

The bread. Matzah is bread made without yeast. When Pharaoh finally said go, Israel left before the dough could rise and carried it in kneading bowls wrapped in their cloaks (12:34, 39). Ancient leaven was a lump of old fermented dough saved from the last batch, a lot like sourdough starter. Clearing all of it out for seven days (12:15) meant a clean break and a fresh start. Many Christians read leaven as a picture of sin, but that comes from Paul, not Exodus. In 1 Corinthians 5:6–8, Paul uses it for a specific case of sexual immorality the church in Corinth was tolerating.

The bitter herbs. Exodus 1:14 says the Egyptians “made their lives bitter with hard service.” The word for “bitter herbs” in 12:8 comes from the same Hebrew root. Every bite tasted like the slavery they were about to leave.

The posture. They ate “with your belt fastened, your sandals on your feet, and your staff in your hand” (12:11). The text doesn’t say they stood, but a staff in hand points that way. By Jesus’ day the posture had flipped. He and his disciples “reclined at table” (Matt 26:20), the way free people ate. Later Jewish tradition required even the poorest person to recline at Passover, and it treated the haste of that first night as belonging only to the Passover in Egypt.

The Memory · Question 6

Israel Kept Forgetting

For a festival God commanded “as a statute forever” (12:14), Israel kept Passover surprisingly rarely.

Outside Exodus, the Old Testament records only a handful of Passovers. Israel kept one in the wilderness (Num 9:1–5) and one when they entered the land under Joshua (Josh 5:10). After that, the record goes quiet for centuries, until the reforms of King Hezekiah (2 Chr 30) and King Josiah (2 Kgs 23:21–23; 2 Chr 35), with one more after the return from exile (Ezra 6:19–22). When Josiah held his Passover, the writer adds that nothing like it had been kept “since the days of the judges” (2 Kgs 23:22). Hezekiah admits the people “had not kept it as often as prescribed” (2 Chr 30:5). Josiah’s Passover came after his workers found a forgotten scroll of God’s law in the Temple (2 Kgs 22:8). Israel had lost track of the very instructions that told them to remember.

Grace built into the rules

God saw that coming and built grace into the rules. Numbers 9:6–12 allowed a make-up Passover a month later for anyone who was ceremonially unclean or traveling, and Hezekiah used that exact provision (2 Chr 30:2). Even then, many people ate without being properly purified. Hezekiah prayed, “May the good LORD pardon everyone who sets his heart to seek God… even though not according to the sanctuary’s rules of cleanness.” The next line says, “And the LORD heard Hezekiah and healed the people” (2 Chr 30:18–20).

Connecting the Dots

Forgetting God is an old problem, and remembering takes effort. God expected his people to drift and built second chances into the very festival meant to keep them anchored. Students who feel like they’ve drifted need to hear that the way back is already open.
The Lamb · Question 7

Did Jesus Eat the Passover or Become It?

The Gospels show both.

Matthew, Mark, and Luke present the Last Supper as a Passover meal. John places Jesus’ death on the day the Passover lambs were being slaughtered. Christians explain the difference in more than one way, and every explanation lands in the same place.

Ate itMatthew · Mark · Luke

In Matthew, Mark, and Luke, the disciples prepare “to eat the Passover” (Mark 14:12), and Jesus says, “I have earnestly desired to eat this Passover with you before I suffer” (Luke 22:15).

Became itJohn

In John, the religious leaders won’t enter Pilate’s headquarters “so that they would not be defiled, but could eat the Passover” (John 18:28), and the crucifixion happens on “the day of Preparation of the Passover” (19:14). John then points out that Jesus’ bones weren’t broken, fulfilling Exodus 12:46 (John 19:36).

There are three common ways to handle this.
1

Some scholars think John arranged his timeline to make a theological point, showing Jesus as the true Passover lamb.

2

Others say historians simply can’t settle which timeline is exact from the evidence we have.

3

Many evangelical scholars argue the accounts fit together, since “Passover” could name the whole week-long festival and “Preparation” was the usual word for Friday.

Whichever view a teacher holds, scholars broadly agree that Passover is the right backdrop for understanding the Last Supper.

It’s ok to say

“The Gospels tell it from different angles, and Christians have explained the timing a few ways. All four agree on the big thing: Jesus died at Passover, and the New Testament calls him our Passover lamb.”

What Jesus said at the table.

Jesus took Passover bread and said, “This is my body” (Luke 22:19). Over the cup he said, “this is my blood of the covenant” (Mark 14:24), echoing the words Moses spoke when God made his covenant with Israel at Sinai (Exod 24:8). In Luke, he calls it “the new covenant in my blood” (Luke 22:20), echoing God’s promise of a new covenant (Jer 31:31). The Supper ties Passover and Sinai to that promise in one meal. Afterward, they sang a hymn and went out to the Mount of Olives (Mark 14:26). Jewish tradition sang Psalms 113–118 at Passover, so that’s very likely what Jesus sang the night he was arrested, including “The stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone” (Ps 118:22).

Connections between the Passover Lamb and Jesus

The strongest connections between the Old Testament and Jesus are the ones the New Testament itself makes, like the unblemished lamb, the unbroken bones, and “Christ, our Passover lamb.” Popular material sometimes goes further, claiming the blood on the doorframe formed a cross or that later Seder customs secretly picture Jesus. Those are devotional readings added much later, and are not part of the original story.

Connecting the Dots

When Jesus wanted his followers to understand the cross, he didn’t start with a lecture. He sat them down at a Passover table, the meal that retold the night a lamb died so God’s people could go free. Then he changed the words. Over the bread he said, “This is my body, which is given for you” (Luke 22:19). Over the cup, “This is my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many” (Mark 14:24). The disciples knew the story by heart: a lamb without blemish, its blood on the door, death passing over, slaves walking out free. Jesus was telling them he was the lamb, and that by the next afternoon his blood would cover them the way the lamb’s blood covered Israel. Every time Christians take Communion, they sit at that same table and remember that someone died in their place so they could go free.
The Lamb · Question 8

From the Passover Table to the Lord’s Table

Jesus started the Lord’s Supper at a Passover meal, and Communion carries some of Passover’s shape without repeating it.

=What carries over.

Both meals are about remembering. God called Passover “a memorial day” (Exod 12:14), and Jesus said, “Do this in remembrance of me” (Luke 22:19). In both, God’s people eat together as the people he rescued, and in both, the meal points forward. Jesus told his disciples he wouldn’t drink the cup again “until that day when I drink it new with you in my Father’s kingdom” (Matt 26:29), and later Jewish tradition looked for God’s final rescue to come on Passover night. Paul says that every time Christians take the Supper, “you proclaim the Lord’s death until he comes” (1 Cor 11:26).

≠What’s different.

Obviously there is no lamb at the Lord’s Table, because Jesus is the lamb. Churches take Communion many times a year, while Passover came once. And when Paul gives instructions for the Supper in 1 Corinthians 11, he doesn’t tie it to Passover. He uses Passover imagery separately, back in chapter 5.

It’s ok to say

“Jesus started Communion at a Passover meal. Passover remembered God bringing Israel out of Egypt, and Communion remembers Jesus’ death and looks forward to his return.”

A word on “Christian Seders.”

Some churches host Passover Seders that present the modern Seder as what Jesus did at the Last Supper. The Seder Jewish families celebrate today, with its set order and its four cups of wine, took shape after the Romans destroyed the Temple in AD 70. Much of it is recorded in the Mishnah, a collection of Jewish teaching from around AD 200. These events can be meaningful, but it’s best not to claim Jesus followed that script. Jewish neighbors still celebrate Passover today, and our language about it should be respectful.

Connecting the Dots

At its heart, Communion is a way of remembering together. When students take it at their home church, they’re doing the same thing Israel did every Passover: sitting at a table God set and retelling a rescue they didn’t earn.
The Takeaway

That night, every house in Egypt had a death in it. The only difference was who died: a firstborn son, or a lamb in his place.

God kept his promise to Abraham to the very day, and centuries later Jesus sat down at a Passover table and used its bread and cup to explain his own sacrifice.
